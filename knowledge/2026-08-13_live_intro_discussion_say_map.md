# LIVE JOURNAL Introduction and Discussion: say / must-not-say map

**Date:** 13 August 2026
**Scope:** Literature and estimand guardrails for the live CHD/HF manuscript. This note does not alter the weather Methods or report any stroke result. “May say” bullets are written in paste-safe academic language. Citation numbers refer to the current live draft where available.

## 1. Goggins et al. 2013: daily AMI admissions, temperature, and pollution

**Source:** Goggins WB, Chan EYY, Yang CY. *Int J Cardiol*. 2013;168:243–249. [doi:10.1016/j.ijcard.2012.09.087](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.ijcard.2012.09.087). **Live citation: [1].**

### May say

- Goggins et al. analysed daily AMI admissions, weather, and air pollution in Hong Kong, Taipei, and Kaohsiung during 2000–2009 [1].
- In Hong Kong, a 1 °C decrease below an estimated 24 °C threshold was associated with a 3.7% increase in AMI admissions using mean temperature over lags 0–13 days [1].
- The study did not identify a statistically significant heat association in the three cities [1].
- Same-day nitrogen dioxide was the strongest pollutant predictor in each city, supporting explicit attention to changing pollution alongside temperature [1].
- The study provides a historical, daily, diagnosis-specific cardiac comparison for a warm-climate city [1].

### Must not say

- Do not call 24 °C a universal, physiological, or present-study threshold.
- Do not say that cold “caused” the present CHD or HF pattern.
- Do not describe the current outcome as AMI or principal-diagnosis cardiac admission; admission cause was not recorded.
- Do not compare the 3.7% daily AMI estimate numerically with a monthly CHD/HF count ratio.
- Do not turn “no statistically significant heat effect” into proof that heat had no effect or that heat has since replaced cold.
- Do not imply that staged pollution analyses in the current archive resolve confounding.

## 2. Goggins et al. 2012: daily haemorrhagic and ischaemic stroke admissions

**Source:** Goggins WB, Woo J, Ho S, Chan EYY, Chau PH. *Int J Biometeorol*. 2012;56:865–872. [doi:10.1007/s00484-011-0491-9](https://doi.org/10.1007/s00484-011-0491-9). **Live citation: [11].**

### May say

- This Hong Kong study analysed daily public-hospital stroke admissions among people aged 35 years or older during 1999–2006, separating haemorrhagic from ischaemic stroke [11].
- Haemorrhagic stroke admissions had an inverse linear association with temperature across the observed range; a 1 °C lower mean over lags 0–4 days was associated with a 2.7% higher admission rate [11].
- The ischaemic stroke association was weaker and apparent below approximately 22 °C; a 1 °C lower mean over lags 0–13 days was associated with a 1.6% higher admission rate [11].
- The haemorrhagic-stroke association was stronger among older people and women, while measured pollutants were not associated with either subtype [11].
- The paper shows that outcome definition and lag window mattered even within an earlier daily stroke series [11].

### Must not say

- Do not imply that the current file contains stroke, stroke subtype, age, or sex results.
- Do not import either stroke coefficient or lag window into the CHD/HF analysis.
- Do not compress distinct haemorrhagic and ischaemic findings into a universal “stroke effect.”
- Do not call a lag-one-month sensitivity equivalent to either daily lag window.
- Do not use the stroke paper as evidence for a CHD-versus-HF mechanism or a later-period regime shift.

## 3. Guo et al. 2024: official hot nights versus excess nighttime heat

**Source:** Guo YT, Chan KH, Qiu H, Wong ELY, Ho KF. *Lancet Reg Health West Pac*. 2024;51:101168. [doi:10.1016/j.lanwpc.2024.101168](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.lanwpc.2024.101168). **Live citation: [17].**

### May say

- Guo et al. analysed **daily**, unplanned emergency admissions through Hong Kong public-hospital accident and emergency departments during the hot seasons of 2000–2019 [17].
- The primary outcome was non-cancer, non-external hospitalisation; circulatory and respiratory admissions and age and socioeconomic groups were also examined [17].
- The official metric, `HNday28`, was a binary day with minimum temperature at least 28 °C. It showed no overall association with hospitalisation over cumulative lags 0–4 days after adjustment for multi-day mean temperature (excess relative risk −0.2%, 95% CI −1.2% to 0.7%) [17].
- Hot-night excess (`HNe`) summed hourly degrees above 28 °C from 20:00 to 07:59. Extreme `HNe` (99th percentile, 28.9 °C·h, versus zero) was associated with 3.1% (1.5% to 4.8%) higher non-cancer, non-external hospitalisation over lags 0–4 days [17].
- Each 10 °C·h increase in `HNe` was associated with 1.9% (0.2% to 3.5%) higher overall hospitalisation and 4.3% (1.8% to 6.9%) higher circulatory hospitalisation [17].
- An intensity-based binary metric, `HNday90th` (`HNe` at or above 17.7 °C·h), was associated with 1.9% (0.7% to 3.0%) higher overall hospitalisation. The comparison supports retaining interpretable official counts while distinguishing duration and intensity [17].

### Must not say

- Do not describe Guo et al. as a monthly study or compare its lag-0–4 estimates with a same-month coefficient.
- Do not call its outcome CHD, HF, stroke, first event, or all-cause hospitalisation; it used emergency non-cancer, non-external admissions and broad cause groups.
- Do not say that the official hot-night definition is useless or always null. The study found no **overall adjusted association** for that binary metric in this design.
- Do not equate the current monthly count of official hot nights with `HNe`; `HNe` requires hourly intensity and a defined 12-hour window.
- Do not attribute the present CHD hot-night pattern to sleep, circulatory strain, socioeconomic position, or any mechanism measured by Guo et al.
- Do not imply that `HNday90th` was the 90th percentile of daily minimum temperature; it was the 90th percentile of positive hourly excess heat.

## 4. Jingwen Liu et al. 2020: daily mortality attributable fractions

**Source:** Liu J, Hansen A, Varghese B, et al. *Sustainable Cities and Society*. 2020;57:102131. [doi:10.1016/j.scs.2020.102131](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.scs.2020.102131). **Live citation: [12].**

### May say

- Liu et al. analysed daily total and cause-specific mortality in Hong Kong during 2006–2016 using a distributed lag non-linear model with quasi-Poisson regression [12].
- The temperature–total mortality association was reported as reversed J-shaped [12].
- The reported overall mortality attributable fraction was 4.72% for cold and 0.16% for heat [12].
- Moderate non-optimum temperatures accounted for a larger mortality attributable fraction than extremes (4.25% versus 0.63%) [12].
- These findings support keeping cold and non-extreme temperature in the local evidence frame rather than treating heat thresholds as the only relevant exposure [12].

### Must not say

- Do not convert either attributable fraction into a CHD, HF, stroke, or hospitalisation coefficient.
- Do not invent the minimum-mortality temperature, moderate/extreme cut-offs, spline knots, degrees of freedom, or complete lag specification.
- Do not call either attributable fraction an incidence proportion or an observed percentage of heat- or cold-coded deaths.
- Do not infer that a larger cold attributable fraction proves a stronger biological cold effect; attributable burden also depends on the exposure distribution and model reference.
- Do not use this paper to claim a genetic, ancestral, or approximately 10 °C mechanism.

## 5. Zhenyuan Liu et al. 2026: model-based excess heat deaths

**Source:** Liu Z, Ren C, Liu J, Kawasaki Y, Bishai DM. *medRxiv*. 2026. [doi:10.64898/2026.03.05.26347683](https://doi.org/10.64898/2026.03.05.26347683). **Preprint. Live citation: [13].**

### May say

- Liu et al. estimated model-based excess heat deaths in Hong Kong during 2014–2023 under four local heatwave scenarios [13].
- The scenarios represented high daily mean temperature with a long lag window, prolonged very hot days, prolonged hot nights, and combined hot days and nights [13].
- The analysis applied published local heat–mortality relative risks to expected age–sex deaths; it did not fit a new daily regression to observed 2014–2023 deaths [13].
- Reported ten-year estimates ranged from 1,455 to 3,238 excess deaths across definitions, showing that exposure definition materially changed the estimated burden [13].
- This preprint complements the present morbidity analysis by translating selected heat–mortality relations into absolute burden [13].

### Must not say

- Do not call these observed heat-coded deaths or observed-minus-forecast mortality.
- Do not import the relative risks, excess-death totals, or demographic patterns into the monthly CHD/HF models.
- Do not imply that all four definitions estimate one interchangeable exposure; they differ in threshold variable, persistence, lag coverage, and transported risk.
- Do not present the preprint as peer reviewed.
- Do not cite a private revision in place of the public preprint or use inconsistent secondary wording to redefine the source heat events.
- Do not call the present count ratios excess deaths or attributable burden.

## 6. Basagaña and Ballester 2024 and 2026: daily effects from aggregated outcomes

**Sources:** Basagaña X, Ballester J. *Lancet Planet Health*. 2024;8:e766–e777. [doi:10.1016/S2542-5196(24)00212-2](https://doi.org/10.1016/S2542-5196(24)00212-2), **live [15]**; Basagaña X, Ballester J. *Epidemiology*. 2026;37:16–20. [doi:10.1097/EDE.0000000000001923](https://doi.org/10.1097/EDE.0000000000001923), **live [16]**.

### May say

- Basagaña and Ballester derived a likelihood that combines temporally aggregated outcomes with disaggregated exposures to estimate an underlying disaggregated association [15].
- In the 2024 mortality simulations, weekly-outcome/daily-exposure estimates were close to daily estimates with little loss of precision; monthly-outcome estimates required substantially more data and were much more variable [15].
- Individual lag estimates from monthly outcomes showed more bias, variability, and sub-nominal coverage than cumulative effects, and therefore required particular caution [15].
- The 2026 study extended the method in simulations to hospital admissions, nitrogen dioxide, and several aggregation schemes; performance improved with longer series and more cases [16].
- The present project tested a constrained implementation in 500 simulations tailored to its weather and monthly outcome setting. The calibration requirements were not met, so the method was not applied to the health series.
- This is a project-specific feasibility result, not a general test of the published method.

### Must not say

- Do not say that the Basagaña–Ballester method fails in general.
- Do not say that monthly outcomes can never support daily-effect recovery; the papers show recovery under sufficiently informative settings.
- Do not say that the published studies validate a daily coefficient for a 132-month CHD/HF series.
- Do not report a real daily CHD or HF coefficient; none was admitted.
- Do not describe the continuity monthly model as a distributed lag non-linear model.
- Do not imply that failure to meet the project’s calibration requirements invalidates the monthly count-ratio analysis, which targets a different estimand.

## 7. Heart failure and cold: local precedent and physiological motivation

**Sources:** Goggins WB, Chan EYY. *Int J Cardiol*. 2017;228:537–542. [doi:10.1016/j.ijcard.2016.11.106](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.ijcard.2016.11.106) (**add to live references if used**); Ikäheimo TM. *Temperature (Austin)*. 2018;5:123–146. [doi:10.1080/23328940.2017.1414014](https://doi.org/10.1080/23328940.2017.1414014) (**add only if physiological wording is retained**).

### May say

- A Hong Kong daily study of public-hospital HF admissions and HF mortality during 2002–2011 found higher risk at lower mean temperatures after adjustment for season, trend, pollutants, and other meteorological variables (Goggins and Chan, 2017).
- For admissions, the reported cumulative relative risk comparing 11 °C with 25 °C was 2.63 (2.43 to 2.84) over lags extending to 23 days; associations were stronger in older groups and for new than recurrent hospitalisations (Goggins and Chan, 2017).
- This disease-specific local evidence is a closer historical motivation for retaining cold in an HF manuscript than AMI or stroke evidence alone.
- Cold exposure can activate sympathetic vasoconstriction, raise peripheral resistance and blood pressure, and increase cardiac workload; these pathways make cold–HF research biologically plausible (Ikäheimo, 2018).
- The present aggregate data do not measure these pathways, so physiology should appear as interpretation or motivation rather than as a result.

### Must not say

- Do not compare the 11 °C-versus-25 °C daily cumulative relative risk with the present per-five-cold-days monthly count ratio.
- Do not call the present event an HF-caused admission; it is the first recorded hospitalisation after first HF diagnosis, without recorded admission cause.
- Do not say that vasoconstriction, blood pressure, afterload, infection, or decompensation mediated the present association.
- Do not use the older study’s “new hospitalisation” category as proof that its outcome construction matches the present first-event algorithm.
- Do not infer an individual response from a territory-month association.
- Do not claim that cold is the only plausible explanation for winter variation.

## 8. Nighttime heat, sleep, and blood pressure: motivation only

**Sources:** Guo et al. 2024, live [17]; Chevance G, Minor K, Vielma C, et al. *Sleep Med Rev*. 2024;75:101915. [doi:10.1016/j.smrv.2024.101915](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.smrv.2024.101915); Ashe N, Wozniak S, Conner M, et al. *Systematic Reviews*. 2025;14:19. [doi:10.1186/s13643-024-02742-7](https://doi.org/10.1186/s13643-024-02742-7). **Add the latter two only if this mechanism paragraph remains.**

### May say

- Higher indoor or outdoor temperature is generally associated with poorer sleep quality and shorter sleep duration, with stronger associations in warm seasons and vulnerable populations (Chevance et al., 2024).
- Hot sleeping environments may impede the nocturnal decline in core temperature required for sleep onset and maintenance (Chevance et al., 2024).
- Sleep disruption, altered autonomic measures, and blood-pressure responses are plausible links between extreme heat and cardiovascular stress, but direct evidence connecting all parts of this pathway is sparse (Ashe et al., 2025).
- A scoping review found only three eligible studies, no cardiovascular disease outcomes, and no formal mediation analysis; two studies reported lower systolic and diastolic blood pressure during extreme heat (Ashe et al., 2025).
- These hypotheses help explain why nighttime intensity deserves study beside a binary hot-night count; they do not identify the mechanism of a monthly association [17].

### Must not say

- Do not say that hot nights raised blood pressure in the present cohort. Blood pressure was not measured, and published heat-related blood-pressure responses are not uniformly upward.
- Do not say that sleep mediated the present CHD association; sleep duration, sleep quality, indoor temperature, and mediation were not measured.
- Do not equate outdoor HKO minimum temperature with bedroom temperature or personal heat exposure.
- Do not claim a causal chain from hot nights to sleep loss to CHD or HF hospitalisation.
- Do not import age or socioeconomic effect modification from Guo et al.; the delivered CHD/HF aggregates have no such strata.
- Do not expand a short motivation sentence into a mechanistic conclusion.

## 9. First-event construction, risk-set depletion, and the days-in-month offset

**Source cue:** Current governed outcome contract and live Methods; ecological inference boundary: Greenland and Morgenstern, *Int J Epidemiol*. 1989;18:269–274, **live [14]**.

### May say

- The outcomes are monthly counts of the first recorded hospitalisation after first CHD diagnosis and after first HF diagnosis among people diagnosed with type 2 diabetes and/or hypertension during 2013–2023.
- A first-event construction removes a person from eligibility for a further first event. The observed decline in annual counts is therefore compatible with risk-set depletion, but also with cohort entry, secular changes, and pandemic-related care disruption.
- Monthly counts of cohort members still at risk were unavailable. The days-in-month offset standardises exposure time only; it does not supply cohort person-time.
- The fitted quantities are ecological monthly count ratios conditional on calendar month and trend, not cohort incidence-rate ratios [14].
- A general-population denominator cannot replace the unobserved diabetes/hypertension cohort risk set.

### Must not say

- Do not call the cohort strictly closed unless entry, censoring, death, migration, and follow-up rules are confirmed by the outcome co-investigator.
- Do not call the annual decline proof of depletion or estimate its proportion without monthly risk-set counts.
- Do not report incidence, hazard, individual risk, or person-time rates.
- Do not imply that the days offset corrects changing cohort size or composition.
- Do not interpret an ecological count ratio as an individual causal effect [14].
- Do not describe the hospitalisation as being caused by CHD or HF when admission cause was not recorded.

## Introduction spine

1. **Thermal coexistence.** Open with cardiovascular morbidity in a humid subtropical city where hot nights have increased but cold days persist. Avoid a generic climate-change preamble.
2. **Historical local daily evidence.** Move from Goggins et al. 2013 AMI and Goggins et al. 2012 stroke to the more outcome-aligned Goggins and Chan 2017 HF study. One sentence should state that these are daily, diagnosis-specific studies from earlier periods.
3. **Why nighttime heat needs more than a binary flag.** Use Guo et al. 2024 to distinguish the official `Tmin ≥28 °C` indicator from hourly excess heat. State its daily hot-season emergency-hospitalisation outcome and the overall `HNday28` null before the positive intensity findings.
4. **Mortality evidence is complementary.** Use Jingwen Liu et al. 2020 for cold-versus-heat and moderate-versus-extreme mortality attributable fractions. Use Zhenyuan Liu et al. 2026 for definition-sensitive, model-based excess heat deaths. Keep both estimands separate from morbidity counts.
5. **Gap and objective.** State that the present question concerns later-period monthly first-hospitalisation counts for CHD and HF in a diabetes/hypertension cohort. End with the paired hot/cold exposure set and the monthly count-ratio estimand.

## Discussion spine

1. **Lead with the inferential limit.** State the complete pattern, multiplicity result, and uncertainty discordance before interpreting individual contrasts.
2. **Make bounded local comparisons.** Compare the cold-HF direction with Goggins and Chan 2017 and the hot-night question with Guo et al. 2024. Compare questions and directions, not coefficient magnitudes.
3. **Keep metric differences visible.** The present official count is not Guo’s hourly `HNe`; a difference between mean temperature and official counts does not identify an intensity mechanism.
4. **Keep mortality studies complementary.** Daily mortality attributable fractions and RR-transported excess deaths cannot be rescaled into monthly morbidity count ratios.
5. **Use physiology sparingly.** Cold-related vasoconstriction and nighttime-heat disruption of sleep or nocturnal recovery are plausible motivations only. State that mediators and personal exposure were not measured.
6. **Close on design limits.** Explain first-event risk-set depletion, absent cohort person-time, monthly ecological resolution, absent admission cause, and the project-specific refusal to recover daily effects. End with hypotheses for better-denominated and temporally resolved data, not a causal thermal allocation.

## Dead ends: do not chase tonight

- Exact minimum-mortality temperature, thermal cut-offs, spline settings, or the complete cause-specific table for Liu et al. 2020 without the full paper and supplement.
- Any stroke coefficient, stroke subtype narrative, or stroke row count; no stroke series was delivered.
- A numerical comparison of daily AMI, stroke, HF, mortality, or hot-night estimates with monthly CHD/HF count ratios.
- A new hourly excess-nighttime-heat analysis or a change to the weather Methods without the weather co-investigator’s definition lock.
- A claim that sleep, blood pressure, infection, pollution, vasoconstriction, or adaptation mediated the current associations.
- Further tuning of the aggregated-outcome daily-recovery method to obtain a real coefficient after the failed 500-replicate calibration.
- A claim that the annual outcome decline quantifies closed-cohort depletion without entry, censoring, and still-at-risk counts.
- Private-manuscript wording when the public preprint or primary heat-definition source is the citable record.

## Three highest-leverage Introduction upgrades

1. **Add the outcome-aligned Hong Kong HF anchor.** Goggins and Chan (2017) supplies direct daily HF admissions evidence for cold, reducing reliance on AMI and stroke as proxies.
2. **Use Guo et al. [17] precisely.** The important contrast is not “hot nights matter”: the official `HNday28` flag had no overall adjusted association, whereas hourly excess heat and its intensity-based binary metric did. The study was daily, hot-season, emergency hospitalisation.
3. **Make the local evidence chain cumulative.** Jingwen Liu et al. (2020) supplies daily mortality attributable fractions; Zhenyuan Liu et al. (2026) supplies definition-sensitive model-based excess deaths; the live paper supplies monthly first-hospitalisation count ratios. One sentence separating these quantities will prevent most overstatement later.
