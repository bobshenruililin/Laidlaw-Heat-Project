# The File That Arrived Was Not the File We Named

Week 1 taught me that a research question can change its mind.

Week 2 taught me that waiting is full of verbs: define, document, resist cooking ninety-eight recipes at once.

Then a workbook arrived.

I had been practising the sentence “when the stroke aggregates come.” Roro’s email even mentioned stroke alongside the other outcomes. What I could actually open were monthly counts of first hospitalisation after a first diagnosis of coronary heart disease, or of heart failure, among people already living with type 2 diabetes and/or hypertension.

Stroke was named. Stroke was not attached.

This is a less cinematic plot twist than AMI leaving the project in July, and a more useful one. The data did not refuse to exist. They refused to be the series I had been rehearsing.

First hospitalisation after first diagnosis is a precise recipe. It is not “how many heart attacks the city had this month.” Admission cause is still absent, so I still cannot honestly say *why* someone was admitted. The event is the first recorded hospital stay after the first CHD or HF diagnosis record in that cohort. After that event, the person leaves the first-event pool.

You can see the recipe in the totals without any temperature model. CHD first-event counts fall from 23,830 in 2013 to 12,323 in 2023. Heart failure falls from 4,336 to 2,296. There is a further dip in 2020. Over the same years the general population aged 35 and older rose by about 17%.

If you came to the project with a stable-risk-set instinct — a Table 1 of people sitting still while the weather moved — this picture will not satisfy it. It is the expected shape of a first-event construction in a closed-ish diagnosis window, plus a pandemic in the middle. It is not a measure of falling incidence in Hong Kong. Calling it incidence would require monthly counts of cohort members still at risk. We do not have those.

So the honest name for the number is a **count ratio**: given calendar month and a smooth function of time, how do this month’s thermal encodings relate to this month’s first-event counts, with days-in-month as the offset.

Quieter than a daily trigger. Still a real question. Still not the question I had been advertising to myself as “stroke, any day now.”

We did run the panel we had promised: many labelled ways of encoding heat and cold, not ten press releases. Official hot nights, very hot days, cold days; monthly means of temperature; spells; lagged months; pollution and influenza as staged companions; a pre-2020 window. Age bands and sex splits never ran, because the file is territory-month, not a miniature census. A stroke subtype split never ran, because there is no stroke file. A severe-cold recipe near 10 °C flagged zero months, which is a useful kind of failure: the discussion prompt is not currently an executable monthly exposure.

After all of that, every core comparison still has a *q*-value above 0.19. Two residual patterns are visible and exploratory: heart-failure counts with official cold days, coronary counts with official hot nights. The second of those depends on how uncertainty is computed, and it is not visible in the years before 2020. That is not a headline. It is what the complete panel looks like when you refuse to filter it.

Hogan’s weather paragraph stays his, including a sentence a grammar checker would like to “fix.” Roro still owns the health-data Methods — ICD lists, timing, inpatient versus other admission types. Professor Bishai still owns whether this exploratory panel becomes a confirmatory claim. My job, this week, is to stop writing as if we were waiting for a file that already arrived, and to keep writing as if stroke might still arrive later without inventing it now.

From the outside, “the data came” looks like the end of waiting.

From the inside, it is the start of a stricter wait: for a denominator that matches the cohort, for a weather definition locked in someone else’s words, for permission to put even a cautious number on a public network, and for a stroke series that was named in an email and has not yet become a column.

The waiting is no longer empty.

It has a workbook in it. The workbook is allowed to be the wrong one, if we are willing to name it correctly.
