# The File Did Not Finish the Paper

Week 5 ended with something to run.

Week 6 began with the quieter half of that sentence. After you run it, you still have to decide what the running was for.

I had imagined that a file plus a labelled panel would produce a Results section in the ordinary way. Numbers would appear. A sentence would follow them. The heading that had sat empty in Week 4 would finally earn its confidence.

What appeared instead was a sorting problem.

There is the Laidlaw report, which is a programme essay with a deadline. There is a manuscript in the repository, which is a methods-heavy draft. And there is a shared document Hogan opened weeks ago, which is the paper the three of us are actually writing. These are related. They are not the same object. A sentence that belongs in one of them can make a mess in another.

Hogan’s weather paragraph is already in the shared file, in his words. The instruction is to leave it there. Roro still owns the health-data methods: what the columns mean, which codes count, whether a hospitalisation is the event we think it is. Professor Bishai still owns whether a complete exploratory panel is allowed to remain exploratory.

My job this week is mostly distinction, paste, and refusal.

Distinction: a programme poster is not a journal figure. A city-wide population number is still not the group of people who can still contribute a first event. A monthly pattern is still not a daily trigger.

Paste: the rest of the instructions, and the first honest Results sentences, belong in Hogan’s file. They do not belong in a parallel document sent overnight as if collaboration were a delivery problem.

Refusal: the remaining recipes stay on the counter. We listed ninety-eight ways a month could be hot or cold. Cooking the rest of them this week would not tell us whether Hogan’s weather definition is locked, whether stroke will arrive, or whether the team is willing to submit without a headline.

The local mortality literature had already warned us that winter is part of the Hong Kong story. The CHD and HF counts, once they existed, had their own quieter warning. A first-event series shrinks as people leave the pool. Official cold days, once you allow for the month of the year, live almost entirely in winter.

None of that is a percentage I can take to a website. It is the shape of the work after the file: looking carefully at what the counts are, and at what they refuse to be.

Code is excellent at some of this. It can check a yearbook against a table. It can draft a methods paragraph. It can list which labelled models ran and which could not, because age bands were absent or because stroke is still a named absence.

It cannot paste into Hogan’s file for me. It cannot sit in the conversation that locks a heatwave recipe. It cannot decide that an exploratory panel is finished. It cannot invent a stroke column.

Week 4 taught me to write into a blank. Week 5 taught me that arrival is not the same as the series I had rehearsed.

Week 6 is less cinematic. After there is something to run, most of the remaining work is still waiting. Only now the waiting has a document in it, and the document has other people’s names on the parts I am not allowed to finish.
