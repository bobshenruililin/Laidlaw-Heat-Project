# Week 2 reflection — A labelled panel, not seventeen discoveries

This week has been one of my busiest so far — and also one of the most clarifying.

The centre of gravity was no longer “how fast can I build pipelines?” It became “how do I show the team what is true now without overselling it?” I prepared a Week 2 progress deck for the lab: less a victory slide deck, more a shared map. Hogan’s weather lead, Roro’s stroke-aggregate path, and Dr Bishai’s multi-method nudge all had to appear as contributions, not as footnotes under my name.

Looking back over papers and meeting notes from the first weeks reminded me how much the project has already evolved. We are not recreating a daily DLNM admissions study. We are preparing a **monthly** question: how thermal exposures from 2013–2023 associate with stroke event aggregates, and how stable that picture is across a shared panel of specifications. Complementary to daily heatwave–mortality work in the lab — not a duplicate of it.

Two figures from this week capture the scientific posture better than any slogan.

First, the climate and pollution layers are ready enough to discuss descriptively. Hot nights and very hot days rose after 2018; cold days persist. Nitrogen dioxide and particulate matter declined across the window while ozone rose — a changing pollutant mix that has to be staged carefully if it enters models later. These are environmental facts, not stroke findings, and saying that sentence out loud matters.

Second, heatwave-month definitions disagree by design. The same 132 months look “hot” under a lenient percentile rule and look rare under a strict combined day–night spell rule. Guo/Gasparrini-style multi-definition thinking, Ren/Wang spells and 2D3N, and Hogan’s idea of counting heatwaves then flagging upper-tail months all belong in one **labelled panel**. The point is stability and honesty under definition uncertainty — not seventeen press releases.

The readiness board is equally instructive. Public layers and analysis plumbing can be green while stroke aggregates remain awaiting. Influenza surveillance (CHP flu) sits in that readiness story for a reason: winter stroke risk can be cold, flu, or both; without flu we risk mis-attributing an epidemic wave to temperature. Leadership here is partly the discipline of the honesty line — ready to discuss environment, outcome logic, and writing; not ready to claim coefficients, “heat replaced cold,” or synthetic dry-runs as results.

Alongside the science, Laidlaw Stage 3 is coming into view: a literature-and-methods essay, an A0 portrait poster, endorsement paperwork, and November showcase spaces. Writing early — as other scholars modelled — is how I stay honest about readiness rather than waiting for a cinematic finale that the data calendar may not provide on cue.

If Week 1 taught me that groundwork counts, Week 2 taught me that **credit and boundaries** are leadership skills. Going fast alone would have meant finishing weather definitions without Hogan or narrating outcome timing without Roro. Going far together means leaving decisions written down, figures labelled as descriptives, and asks that are small enough for busy collaborators to answer.

Things build over time. My job is to keep the gradual blocks productive — so that when results do arrive, the sudden part is analysis under a shared plan, not a scramble to invent certainty.
